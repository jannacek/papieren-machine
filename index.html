A language model you can hold

Every card in this machine says one thing only: after this word came that word.

The cards are copied by hand from Dutch newspapers printed before 1880, from the KB’s Delpher. Sort enough of them into trays and the pile can speak: draw a card blind, read the next word, look that word up, draw again. What comes out sounds like an 1840s newspaper and means nothing. Everyone in the room knows exactly where each word came from.

The machine looks exactly one word back. That makes it a bigram model, the smallest language model there is. In 1948 Claude Shannon built the same thing with a book and a pencil and published it as a “second-order word approximation” of English. Chatbots make the same kind of prediction, but look thousands of words back and keep what they learned as numbers instead of cards.

Three stations, one machine

Each station is one step of how a language model gets made, done by hand and slowly enough to see the decisions.

Groups of four wait at the reading stand, get collected, and receive a clipboard. The clipboard says which version they are: v01, v02, up to v10. Every group trains its version on top of the cards of the groups before it, so the machine knows a little more each time. Then they work through the stations below.

1

De bronThe source: choose, label, write out

  1. Pick a newspaper page and a short sentence, 12 words at most.
  2. Decide what kind of text it is: one of five colours.
  3. Copy the sentence as word pairs, one pair per card, from full stop to full stop. A full stop and a comma are words too.
  4. Write your version on every card and fill in the model card.

In a real model: corpus, labels, tokenisation

You chose the data. Someone has to say what kind of data it is. Someone decided a comma is a word. The house rules here: lowercase, numbers in letters, spelling as printed, nothing corrected. “Someone decided them; these, I decided.”

You decide

Newspapers more than 140 years old contain racist and colonial language, advertisements included. Copy such a sentence or leave it out? Whatever goes in, the machine may later say aloud. Participants decide and note it on their model card.

2

De machineFeed the machine: sort, count

  1. Look at the first word on your card.
  2. Does that word have its own tray? Stand the card in it, at the back.
  3. If not, file it in the catalogue, A–Z, with the cards that start with the same word.
  4. Is your pair already there? Add it anyway. Every card counts.

In a real model: training

Training is counting what follows what. The previous word decides which tray you open, and inside it every card counts: if stad followed three times and zee once, the machine will say stad three times as often. It understands nothing. It only remembers the word before.

3

De machine spreektThe machine speaks: draw blind, word after word

  1. Take a strip. Write the version and the time on it.
  2. Start at the “.” tray, because every sentence starts after a full stop. Eyes closed, take one card.
  3. Write the word after the arrow in the first box and colour the dot in the card’s colour.
  4. Put the card back. Look up the word you just wrote, draw again, repeat.
  5. At a full stop the sentence is done. Read it aloud, hang it on the line, take a card home.

In a real model: sampling

Do your dots change colour halfway? Your sentence jumped from one kind of text to another, exactly at a word both of them use.

    The print kit

    Everything the room touches is designed, bilingual and ready to print: print set V4.4, 25 October 2026.

    One visual system runs through all of it: five pastel colours for five kinds of text, a mono face for the machine and a serif for the human voice, on black and paper. It is printed in colour on 120–250 g paper and cut by hand or on a guillotine.

    The model card

    Each clipboard sheet is a model card, the documentation real AI models come with. It records the version, what it builds on, the training data (newspaper and date), the kind of text, the number of cards, what was left out on purpose, the knowledge cutoff (the year of the newspaper), the known limitations, and who built it. Under known limitations it says: remembers only the previous word, cannot read, repeats word for word where it knows little, never says a word nobody wrote first.

    Dry run: four groups, four versions

    Before anyone touches paper, the whole procedure was run digitally to see whether it holds.

    This is a simulation. Sixteen simulated participants worked on three real newspaper pages. It tests the procedure; it is not a measured live workshop. Outputs were kept as first sampled, without reselection.

    Format

    ProposalThree hours for 16 people at four tables, in the KB Atelier.

    Print setAn evening workshop, 19:45–21:45, after the 19:15 talk, in the Erasmuszaal. Groups of four are collected one after another.

    Stages

    ProposalFour: corpus, tokenise, label, predict.

    Print setThree stations: de bron (choose, label, write out), de machine (feed it), de machine spreekt (let it speak).

    Labelling

    ProposalFour labellers on one document: three historical KB vocabularies and a 2026 data annotator with twenty seconds and no field for “I don’t know”.

    Print setOne gesture: pick one of five colours for the kind of text. Not sure? Choose anyway. That is labelling.

    The newspaper

    ProposalTables read a column through a paper mask, transcribe it, and cut printed copies into word-pair scraps.

    Print setParticipants highlight one sentence of up to 12 words on a printed page, in the colour for its kind of text, and copy it as word pairs onto cards.

    Memory

    ProposalEach table builds its own model from its own 30 pages.

    Print setOne machine that grows: v01 to v10, each group on top of the last, every card stamped with its version.

    What is lost

    ProposalA discard box all evening, emptied onto the floor and pegged across the Atelier window.

    Print setTwo named silences, a model card that states what was left out on purpose, and a line of the sentences the machine said.

    Where it sits in the doctorate

    Participatory artistic situations can make AI and data systems perceptible, interpretable and open to situated response.Research framing, from the doctoral trajectory

    The doctorate is a Professional Doctorate in Arts + Creative at Hanze University of Applied Sciences (2023–2027). In the portfolio it is called AI & Data Literacy through Artistic Research Environments. It works through four participatory settings, the AT-LABs: body and health, classroom, city, and planet. Each one inherits what the one before it taught, and each follows the same cycle of design, intervention, reading, reflection and feed-forward.

    The Paper Machine takes that method into a public room. A language model is a chain of human decisions: which text goes in, how it is cut into units, what kind of text it counts as, what comes next. Here a room of festival visitors makes each of those decisions with a highlighter and a pencil, on the library’s own newspapers, slowly enough to see them. They leave with a sentence the machine said and a model card that records what it was built from and what was left out. The point is not that AI is a library. It is that both are made of choices someone made and then stopped being able to see.

    What the format asks is whether people with no AI background, and mostly no Dutch, can read a system by making a small one. Not understanding the Dutch is deliberate: it is the most accurate simulation of the thing being studied, because a model does not read, it counts what follows what. It is a new format, designed for this festival, and its first run is on 29 October. What it shows is still to be learned.
